Some tend to dislike green since there is almost none of it in the universe. 
The culprits are the black body radiation curve and the stars themselves. 
Even though lots of stars peak around green, they are also intense in blue and red. 
      
***And that is white to us.***

Some greenhorns — sorry guys, we've all been here :) — might get spooked by the first stretch of their first OSC session that comes out totally green. 
It is caused by the RGB Bayer mask that contains more green pixels than red and blue. Thus the signal is stronger in the green channel. 

A similar situation arises with narrowband SHO images, where we often cram Ha (usually by far the strongest channel) into the green channel for a similar outcome to OSC.

***And that might lead to a slight green PTSD.***

But. Green is as good a colour as any other. We do massage our data to a great extent anyway. It is especially true for narrowband. 
But RGB shots are not always kept as close to reality as possible either. 

***And that is fine. We are mostly not scientists. We are peepers and we do it for looks and peeps :)***

#### Note on SCNR — The Great Green Obliterator
I was doing it too — just run it and the green is gone.

The default *Average Neutral* method computes G' = min(G, ½(R+B)) - meaning it pulls the green channel down where green exceeds the average of red and blue.
If used properly it works fine, because green is naturally weak. But if the image is in an imbalanced state and green contains legitimate signal, it simply 
causes signal loss. 

It is always better to rein in the green with proper colour calibration or narrowband normalisation.

***Green isn't evil and can be part of our toolkit to express something.***
